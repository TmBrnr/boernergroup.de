“The data stays in Germany.”

In conversations about AI, that sentence is often offered as if it settles the question. For me, it is where the conversation starts.

Of course location matters. It matters especially when public authorities or companies handle sensitive information. But an address in Frankfurt is one fact about a system, not a verdict on who controls it.

Who can access it? Who decides which model runs, and when it changes? Who installs updates? What happens if the provider changes its service or stops supporting it? Those questions tell us more about our independence than a server address does.

## Location is a factor, not a sovereignty test

The European Commission’s Cloud Sovereignty Framework makes the distinction clear: it considers dimensions including legal jurisdiction, data and AI, operations, technology and supply chains. The framework has been used in a Commission procurement; it is not a universal certification or a rule for every AI product. But it is a useful reminder that sovereignty is made up of several questions, not one. ([European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/sovereign-cloud-framework-explained-2026-06-01_en))

A German data centre can tell you where certain data is stored or processed. It cannot, on its own, tell you who administers the service, which subcontractors are involved, how changes are approved or what you can take with you if you leave. Those answers depend on the service, the provider and the contract.

That is where I would start asking questions. Can the customer choose or replace the model? Can it delay an update or return to a known version? Does the system need a continuing connection to an external service? Who keeps it running if the provider’s support is unavailable?

## Who holds the keys?

A system can run in Germany and still leave important decisions somewhere else. Perhaps only the provider can install a new model version. Perhaps the customer can export its records but not the workflows, integrations or configurations built around them.

That may be fine while everything is working. The invoices arrive, the support team responds and the next update looks routine. The dependency becomes easier to see when the provider’s interests and the customer’s needs diverge.

A product might be discontinued. Its price or terms might change. The provider might be acquired, or decide to change how it serves a particular customer. These are scenarios to plan for, not predictions about any one supplier. The point is that a contract and an architecture determine how much room the customer has when circumstances change.

## I want to know how we get out

Anyone buying an AI system should ask as much about leaving as about getting started.

Can we replace the model without rebuilding the application? Can we retrieve our data in a usable format? What happens to our configurations, integrations and processing workflows? Who else could operate the system, and how long would a transition take?

The EU Data Act gives customers of covered data-processing services switching and export rights. It has applied since 12 September 2025. But those rights do not make every AI component portable: the rules define what counts as exportable, and assets protected by intellectual-property rights or trade secrets can be excluded. The ordinary maximum transition period is 30 calendar days; where that is technically infeasible, a provider can set a justified alternative period of up to seven months. ([EU Data Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/2854/oj))

That is useful progress. It is not a promise that an organisation can reproduce its entire AI application, workflows and operating environment somewhere else in a month. Buyers still need to establish what their specific service lets them export and test whether the result is usable.

If switching is possible only in theory, or would take years and a multimillion-euro rebuild, the dependency is real. A credible exit plan belongs in any serious purchasing decision. It need not anticipate every possible failure. Someone should have thought it through before the organisation commits for years.

## Europe does not have to build everything itself

I do not believe Europe needs to recreate every technical component within its borders. We will continue working with international partners. In many areas, that makes sense and may be necessary.

But we should know where we depend on them and what those dependencies mean for the work at hand. If a copywriting tool becomes unavailable, there may be an easy workaround. A system used in daily public-sector or security operations calls for a different standard. The customer needs to know who can access it, who approves changes and how operations continue when something fails.

A European logo answers those questions no better than a German server address. I would judge a European provider by the same standard as any other: what control does it actually give its customers?

## Settle this before signing

These are procurement questions, not a claim that every test is required by law. They belong in product selection, contracts and acceptance testing.

Ask the provider to demonstrate a data export. Test whether a model can be replaced. Inspect access permissions. Agree who is responsible for keeping operations running during an outage. Check the provisions for change notices, discontinuation, termination and transition.

That takes time. It forces providers to be precise. And it may mean an impressive demo is not enough to win the contract.

I am comfortable with that. If we want to use AI for important work in Europe, we need the ability to assess it, operate it and replace it when necessary. Otherwise, we are buying a system whose essential terms remain outside our hands.

A data centre in Frankfurt is a good start. My next question is: can we keep working if the provider says no tomorrow?
